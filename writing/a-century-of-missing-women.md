Every ten years, for over a century, India has counted itself. And every ten years the count has come back with the same quiet asymmetry: fewer women than men. In 1901 the census recorded 972 women for every 1,000 men. By 1991 it recorded 927. The number has never once, in that stretch, returned to where it began.

A sex ratio is one of the least glamorous numbers a state produces. It is not growth, not deficit, not a headline. It is simply a count of who is here. And precisely because it is so basic, it is hard to argue with — which is what makes its long decline so damning.

## The audit that named it

The decline was not discovered late. In 1974, the government&rsquo;s own Committee on the Status of Women in India laid it out plainly in a report called *Towards Equality* — the first full audit of Indian measurement conducted, in effect, by its subjects.<sup><a href="#fn1" id="ref1">1</a></sup> The committee noticed something that the individual census tables had obscured: the ratio was not merely low, it was *falling*, census after census, and it had been falling for seventy years.

That reframing matters. A single low number invites a shrug — perhaps women are simply undercounted. A seventy-year slope invites a question: what is the machinery doing?

## What a count can hide

Part of the answer is that a sex ratio is not one number but the sum of many decisions. Who counts as resident. How infants are recorded. Whether a household reports its daughters as readily as its sons. Each is a small act of measurement, and each can lean.

The economist Amartya Sen gave the accumulated lean a name in 1990: *missing women* — the women who would be alive if girls and women received the same care, nutrition and medical attention as boys and men.<sup><a href="#fn2" id="ref2">2</a></sup> Applied to India, the arithmetic turned a demographic curiosity into a moral one. The gap between the ratio India had and the ratio it might have had was not a rounding error. It was tens of millions of people.

## The recovery, and its caveat

After 1991 the line turns. The ratio climbs to 933 in 2001 and 943 in 2011 — a genuine, if partial, recovery. It would be easy to read the upturn as the story resolving itself.

It is worth resisting that reading. An all-India average can rise while the number that alarms demographists most — the *child* sex ratio, girls to boys among the very young — moves the other way, as sex-selective practices concentrate at the beginning of life rather than across it. The headline can recover while the leading edge deteriorates. Which is, in miniature, the argument of the book this page belongs to: an average is a decision about what to foreground, and averages are very good at burying their own worst news.

The line at the top of this essay is not a natural fact. It is an artefact — of registration, of definition, of a century of choices about whom to count carefully. That it can be drawn at all is a small triumph of the Indian statistical system. That it slopes downward for most of its length is the system telling us something it was built, almost despite itself, to reveal.
